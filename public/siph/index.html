The Silicon
Photonic Chip

A silicon photonic IC does not move electrons down a copper trace to carry a bit. It moves electrons once — into a laser, into a modulator, into a detector — and lets a waveguide carry the information at the speed of light in silicon, silicon nitride and silicon dioxide. This guide walks the whole stack: the wave, the wafer, the guide, the devices, the coupling that keeps the photons off the chip, the PDK and foundry that make it repeatable, and the test that proves it.

01

Why the wire ran out of road

Skin effect · dielectric loss · Serdes reach · the optical switch point

A copper channel is a distributed low-pass filter. As the symbol rate climbs, three mechanisms eat the eye diagram at once. Conductor loss grows because the skin depth shrinks — at 56 GHz the current in copper flows in roughly 0.28 µm, so the effective resistance rises with √f. Dielectric loss grows linearly with frequency and with the loss tangent of the laminate, and it never stops growing. Radiation and via resonances add notches that no equaliser can undo.

The result is a reach that collapses rather than declines. A FR-4 channel that carries 10 GbE across a whole board is down to a few centimetres of loss budget at 112 Gb/s PAM4 — roughly the distance from a Serdes pin to the near edge of a 60 mm die package. Once you need to cross a rack, copper is not a slow option; it is not an option.

Light in a dielectric waveguide has none of these problems. There is no skin effect because there is no metal carrying the signal; the loss is a flat dB per metre set by scattering and material absorption, not a dB per √GHz. A single-mode silicon nitride waveguide carries 200 Gb/s PAM4 and 400 Gb/s coherent symbols with the same attenuation it had at 1 Gb/s. And because the carrier is optical, many wavelengths share one waveguide — the multiplexing that made fibre networks what they are.

The catch is equally physical: silicon is an indirect-gap semiconductor and cannot lase, and every optical link needs three energy conversions — electrical→optical, optical→routing, optical→electrical. Silicon photonics is the discipline of making those three conversions cheap, small and manufacturable, and of treating everything in between as a passive, wavelength-agnostic network.

The switch point. Optics wins when electrical reach cost exceeds laser-and-modulator cost. In scale-up fabrics that threshold has moved inside the rack: co-packaged optics puts the engine 1–5 cm from the switch die instead of 1–5 m away at a faceplate.

02

Six materials and a bandgap

Why silicon · the two transparent windows · the platform comparison
Every photonic material is a transparency window under a loss cliff. Photons below the band edge pass; photons above it are eaten in microns. Silicon's cliff is at 1107 nm, which is why 1310 and 1550 nm are the working wavelengths of the whole industry.

Two windows, two jobs

O-band 1260–1360 nm is where standard fibre has near-zero chromatic dispersion — the easy choice for PAM4 and for microring filters that must not be chirped. C-band 1530–1565 nm is where EDFA gain, DWDM grids and the lowest fibre loss (0.18 dB/km) live.

Two-photon absorption

Above ~1450 nm, silicon starts absorbing two 1550 nm photons at once. It caps the power you can put into a silicon modulator or amplifier, and it is the main reason high-power amplification moves to InP or to hybrid integration.

Silicon photonics is not a choice about light. It is a choice about manufacturing. The premise is simple and it has held for two decades: if the photonic devices are drawn with the same lithography, etched with the same plasmas, measured with the same metrology and shipped through the same supply chain as a logic chip, then photonics inherits the cost, the yield learning-curve and the 300 mm wafer throughput of CMOS.

The physics it inherits is a trade. Silicon has the highest refractive index of the CMOS-compatible materials (n ≈ 3.48 at 1550 nm), which buys a mode field of a few hundred nanometres and bend radii of a few microns — circuits dense enough to put a 64-channel DWDM demux in a 200 × 200 µm square. The same high index is the source of the platform's two chronic diseases: a 2 % index step at the Si/SiO₂ boundary makes the sidewall roughness of a dry etch scatter light out of the guide, and it makes every facet a 93 % reflector that must be engineered away before a fibre can be attached.

So a real product is never "just silicon". It is a heterogeneous stack: silicon for dense passive routing and fast carrier-based modulators; silicon nitride for low-loss routing, high-Q resonators and power handling; silicon dioxide as cladding and as a stress engine; an RF-loss metal stack above; and III-V material — InP for lasers, sometimes GaAs or InGaAs for detectors — brought in by die attach, wafer bonding or selective regrowth because nothing on a silicon wafer will lase. The 3D model at the top of this page puts that whole stack, and every device on it, into one picture you can take apart.

Why silicon cannot lase. Silicon's conduction-band minimum is offset in k-space from the valence-band maximum, so an electron–hole annihilation needs a phonon to conserve momentum. Radiative recombination is ~10⁶ times slower than in a direct-gap material and non-radiative paths win. Every "silicon laser" is a III-V die, a bonded III-V layer, or a Raman/Er-doped effect — never bulk silicon.

03

Guiding the mode

Total internal reflection · V-number · single-mode cut-off · dispersion · loss budget

A waveguide is a region of high index surrounded by lower index, and it works because a ray hitting that boundary past the critical angle θc = asin(n₂/n₁) is entirely reflected. For Si/SiO₂ that critical angle is 16.8° — a very forgiving guide that can turn a corner in a few microns. The wave picture replaces the ray: only certain field patterns, the modes, are self-consistent under reflection, and each has an effective index n_eff lying strictly between cladding and core index.

Confinement is set by the normalized frequency. For a slab of thickness d, single-mode behaviour requires the guide to be thin and narrow; a 220 nm-thick, 450–500 nm-wide silicon strip supports exactly one quasi-TE and one quasi-TM mode. Widen it to 800 nm and a second lateral mode appears — and with it the disaster of mode beating, where two modes with a Δn_eff of ~10⁻² drift in relative phase with wavelength and temperature and shred any interferometric or coherent link.

The same geometry decides dispersion. Material dispersion in silicon is normal (positive D) near 1550 nm, but a tightly confined mode has strong waveguide dispersion of the opposite sign, so the total can be pushed through zero anywhere from 700 nm to 2 µm by changing width. Sweep a 500 nm strip and watch the sign flip: this zero-crossing is what makes silicon nitride and silicon suitable for Kerr comb generation.

Loss comes from three places and can only be fought in the first two. Side-wall scattering scales as σ²/H³ × the fraction of power in the sidewalls — which is why wider, weaker confinement (SiN, or a larger mode) is dramatically lower loss, and why hydrogen anneal and thermal reflow are fab levers. Bend radiation scales exponentially with 1/R. Two-photon and free-carrier absorption scale with intensity, and are the price of the small mode.

Quasi-TE vs quasi-TM

In a thin, wide core the two polarisations are far from degenerate: TE (E mostly along the wide axis) sits at higher n_eff and is used for most devices. TM is smaller-mode and more lossy at bends. Polarisation diversity or a rotator is mandatory in coherent designs.

The slot and the nanobeam

A 100 nm slot between two strips squeezes the D-field into the low-index gap: 10–40 % of the power in air or water. That is the sensing and nonlinear-boost trick. The inverse — a sub-wavelength grating with an effective index engineered below the cladding — is the standard mode converter and the standard way to make a "leaky" but single-mode, low-loss routing segment.

04

Putting a laser on a wafer that cannot lase

Heterogeneous · transfer-printed · monolithic · micro-transfer · external

The laser is the single hardest integration problem in silicon photonics, and the industry settled on five answers rather than one. They differ in how the III-V gets onto the wafer, and that choice dictates the noise, the reliability, the cost of test, and whether you can harvest a bad die.

Flat-pack heterogeneous is the volume workhorse: InP die are cleaved, metallised and tested at wafer level on a donor, then pick-and-placed onto a photonics wafer with solder or Au–Au pads and underfilled. The laser is a discrete DFB or EML die butted to a silicon waveguide with a mode-size converter, or flip-chip mounted above a grating. You get known-good-die, full III-V performance, and standard back-end handling. The cost is a CTE mismatch between InP and silicon that hammers solder joints through thermal cycling, and a placement tolerance of a few hundred nanometres for butted couplings.

Transfer printing / smart-cut moves a thin membrane of InP (with its quantum wells) onto the silicon wafer, then defines a continuous gain section that is patterned in one litho step with the rest of the circuit. No pick-and-place, no underfill, sub-micron alignment for free — and the gain section can be hundreds of microns long with a phase-tunable reflectivity section, which is how sub-100 Hz linewidths are reached. The risk is the dielectric bond interface: voids kill the device, and defect density in the bonded membrane sets reliability.

Monolithic growth regrows InP (and sometimes GaAs) directly into a shallow trench etched in the silicon, in an epitaxy tool, at 500–650 °C. Everything is in-plane, self-aligned and dense enough for hundreds of lasers per die — the path taken for optical I/O in accelerator packages. The engineering tax is severe: thermal cycling of the whole wafer, lattice mismatch at the trench walls, and contamination of the epi chamber by the photonics wafer.

µTP micro-transfer printing is the manufacturing answer to yield: an elastomer stamp stamps thousands of tiny pre-tested laser dies per second with ~1 µm accuracy, and each die's electrical and optical performance can be re-probed after printing. Non-defective ones are re-stamped; the rest are replaced.

External / near-package lasers sidestep integration entirely: a reliable, thermally isolated InP laser sits on the substrate edge, in a CAGE, or on the far side of a fibre, and light is coupled in through an edge or grating coupler. It is the most reliable architecture because the laser is no longer on the hottest die in the package — and reliability, not bandwidth, is what killed the first generation of integrated lasers.

The laser tax. In a first-generation SiPh transceiver the laser can be 30–40 % of the bill of materials and 100 % of the early-life failure rate. Every integration scheme above is an attempt to move that cost and that failure mode somewhere cheaper.

What actually fails

III-V lasers on silicon fail through dark-line defects propagating into the wells under high current density, and through solder-joint fatigue from the CTE mismatch (InP 4.6, Si 2.6, substrate 17 ppm/K). Both are accelerated by junction temperature, which is why the laser wants to be far from the DSP.

Locking it down

A SiPh laser must be wavelength-locked to a gas cell (HCN or acetylene at 1530–1550 nm) or to an on-chip ring reference, because silicon's dn/dT ≈ 1.86×10⁻⁴ /K drags every resonator on the die by ~11 GHz/K. Without a lock, a 30 K thermal excursion walks a 100 GHz channel plan off its filters.

05

Writing bits onto a wave

Plasma dispersion · VπL figure of merit · travelling wave · microring · PAM4 · linear drives

Silicon has no useful Pockels coefficient — it is centrosymmetric, so χ⁽²⁾ vanishes in the bulk. Modulation therefore comes from changing the free-carrier population in the region where the light lives. Injecting electrons and holes into the intrinsic region of a reverse-biased PN junction simultaneously shifts the real index down and the absorption up, via the plasma dispersion effect:

Δn ≈ −[8.8×10⁻⁴·ΔN + 8.5×10⁻¹⁸·ΔP²]
Δα ≈ 8.5×10⁻¹⁸·ΔN + 6.0×10⁻¹⁸·ΔP   (cm⁻¹, Δ in cm⁻³)

The two-carrier asymmetry is the whole design problem: electrons are ~3× more effective per unit index change than holes, but holes dominate the capacitance because they pile up at the junction. Push carrier density too hard and the chirp (α-factor ≈ 1–3 for a dispersion-modulated MZI) broadens the spectral linewidth and detonates the fibre's chromatic-dispersion penalty at 10 km. The engineering answer is to run the junction at a modest Δn and buy the phase shift with length — which is exactly what the VπL figure of merit captures.

A good 2020s silicon MZ modulator: VπL ≈ 1.5–2.5 V·cm, electrode-band-limited to 50–70 GHz, so a 3 mm device lands at ~5 V drive with a 60 GHz BW — enough for 112 Gb/s PAM4 with a linear TIA and DSP equalisation. The travelling-wave geometry is a 50 Ω coplanar or grounded CPW that must be velocity-matched to the RF and impedance-matched to the driver, and its microwave loss is what ultimately caps the device.

Depletion rather than accumulation is the trick that raised the ceiling: reverse-bias a junction so carriers are swept out of the depletion region, and the device becomes majority-carrier, ~10× faster, lower-loss and far more linear — the same physics that makes a photodiode fast. Carrier-depletion MZ and microring modulators both exploit it.

The microring modulator is the bandwidth-per-watt and area winner: a 5–10 µm ring, reverse-biased across its gap, driven ±0.8 V, modulating the resonance edge. It is 1000× smaller than an MZI, and it is naturally WDM — one laser, one waveguide, N rings at N wavelengths. It is also thermally and wavelength-fragile, chirps hard, and its small thermal mass means the drive waveform itself heats the cavity. Every microring product carries an heater-and-feedback loop as a first-class part of the design.

The linearity problem. A ring's transfer function is exponentially shaped; a MZ's is sinusoidal. Both are the wrong shape for PAM4, which is why modern links run PAM4 with a linear driver, a linear TIA and look-up-table pre-distortion, and why "linear-drive" (LPO/LRO) variants that delete the DSP retimer are such a large power saving that the industry is re-engineering modulator linearity around them.

Catching the photon

Ge on Si · UTC · responsivity · dark current · NEP · coherent 90°

Silicon is transparent at 1310 and 1550 nm — its bandgap cuts at 1107 nm. Every photodetector on a silicon photonic chip is therefore a foreign material sitting in a hole in the silicon: germanium (Eg 0.66 eV, cuts at 1880 nm) lattice-mismatched by 4 % and grown by UHV-CVD, or InGaAs/InP bonded or butt-coupled. Germanium on silicon is the volume answer because it can be grown after the transistor flow, in a back-end-of-line module, at 350–450 °C.

The dislocation density of that relaxed Ge layer (~10⁷–10⁸ cm⁻²) would murder a laser but is survivable in a detector, because a detector only needs to separate carriers, not emit light. Threading dislocations are pushed out of the active region by SiN mask-defined selective growth and by SiGe composition-graded buffers, and the residual ones are made harmless by keeping the depletion region thin.

Responsivity is a fight between quantum efficiency and speed. A 400 nm-thick Ge layer absorbs ~90 % of a 1550 nm photon only if the light passes through it — vertically illuminated detectors need a reflector or a cavity; waveguide detectors use an evanescent or butt-coupled geometry so the mode grazes the Ge over 5–15 µm. A vertical Ge PD reaches R ≈ 0.95 A/W at 1310 nm with BW > 50 GHz; waveguide-integrated UTC PDs reach R ≈ 1.0 A/W at >70 GHz.

The UTC (uni-travelling-carrier) photodiode is the speed architecture: an n⁺ / i / p⁺ stack where photogenerated electrons drift across the depletion region at saturation velocity while the slow holes are absorbed in a thin p⁺ layer within a transit time. Everything about it — separate absorption and charge layers, a field that never collapses, small junction capacitance — is designed so that the RC limit and the transit-time limit do not overlap. Space-charge saturation at high photocurrent (the "3 dB compression point", typically +8 to +14 dBm) is what sets the maximum optical power and hence the link's sensitivity margin.

Noise has three terms and only two matter: shot noise from dark current and from the photocurrent itself, and thermal noise from the load and the TIA. Dark current in a good Ge PD is < 1 nA/µm² at −1 V but doubles every ~8–10 °C, which is why detector bias and temperature are controlled in the same loop as the laser. NEP for a direct-detection pin is ~10⁻¹⁴ W/√Hz; for a coherent receiver, the local oscillator makes the LO shot noise dominant and the sensitivity approaches the quantum limit at the cost of a laser, a 90° hybrid and two balanced detectors per polarisation.

Why coherent. A coherent receiver detects field, not power: mixing the incoming signal with a local oscillator in a 90° hybrid gives I and Q per polarisation, so DSP can undo chromatic and polarisation-mode dispersion entirely. That is how 400G-ZR reaches 80–120 km on one fibre pair — and it is why silicon photonics' love of balanced, matched, phase-stable structures pays off in the coherent receiver, which is arguably the best-matched product to the platform.

Monitors are never free

Every closed loop needs a tap: a directional coupler, a weak grating, or the leakage current of an unused detector port. Each tap is insertion loss and a noise source, and monitor PDs are Ge too — so a monitor's dark current drifts with the same thermal budget as the signal path.

The TIA is half the device

A 0.9 A/W detector into a 50 Ω TIA with 4 fF/√Hz input noise lands at ~−22 dBm sensitivity for 100G-PAM4. Gain-bandwidth, input-referred capacitance (which adds directly to the PD's) and the ESD clamp's parasitic all set the eye — co-design the PD mesa and the TIA input stage or don't ship.

07

Splitting, combining, filtering

MMI · Y-branch · MZI · ring filters · AWG · MUX/DEMUX · switches

Every passive function on a photonic chip is interference in disguise. A multimode interference (MMI) splitter is a short slab of wide multimode waveguide: launch light off-centre and it excites a set of supermodes with different propagation constants; those beats re-form an image of the input at periodic self-imaging planes, and a device cut to L = 3·(2M+1)·L₀/8 produces a clean 1×2 or 1×4 split. MMIs are broadband (±40 nm), compact (tens of microns), and robust to ±20 nm of lithography error — which is why they, and not directional couplers, are the default splitter in a PDK.

A directional coupler instead phase-matches two guides for a length Lc and swaps power sinusoidally. It is the tunable, the tap, and the building block of a microring's drop port — but its split ratio is exquisitely sensitive to gap width, so a 3 dB coupler is a calibration item and a 1 % coupler is a fab metric.

An MZI is two splitters and a differential phase: transfer ∝ cos²(Δφ/2). Sweep Δφ with wavelength and you get a free spectral range FSR = λ²/(ngΔL). Interleave two MZIs with a 2:1 ΔL ratio and you get an interleaver — the 50 GHz/100 GHz filter that separates adjacent DWDM channels with no moving parts.

A microring is a resonator: a bus waveguide evanescently coupled to a ring of circumference L, resonant when ng·L = mλ. Through-port dips and drop-port peaks appear at the resonances; the sharpness is the Q, set by the round-trip loss and the coupling regime. Drive it to critical coupling (κ = α) and the through port goes to zero at resonance; past that is over-coupling, which modulators prefer for its flatter, more linear edge.

An AWG (arrayed waveguide grating) is the wavelength multiplexer: a free-form region (the Rowland circle), N arms with a fixed length step ΔL, and a second focusing slab. Path-length delay turns wavelength into angle, and angle into output port. A 40-channel 100 GHz AWG is ~500 × 400 µm, has ±10 dB of passband flatness issues at the edges, and its centre wavelengths walk at ~11 pm/K — so it needs an athermal cladding (polymer with negative dn/dT) or a heater tree.

Design rule. MMIs for splitting, MZIs for filtering and switching, rings for dense WDM and modulation, AWGs for many channels with one device, and sub-wavelength gratings for anything that needs an index you cannot otherwise buy. Every one of them is a phase device, so every one of them is a thermal and foundry-variation problem before it is an optical one.

Thermo-optic switching

Si's dn/dT is positive and large: 1.86×10⁻⁴ /K. A 1 mW heater under a silicon waveguide shifts neff by ~10⁻³ — a π phase shift for pennies and no leakage. The cost is crosstalk (heaters heat neighbours), ms speed, and DC power that never goes away.

Carrier switching

Free carriers switch in ns with ~fJ, but they add loss and chirp. The modern pattern is thermal for the set-point, carrier for the swing: a heater parks the operating point, the junction does the high-speed excursion.

Athermal designs

Clad the interferometer arm in a polymer with dn/dT ≈ −3×10⁻⁴ /K and balance the two arms' lengths so the temperature coefficients cancel. A 50 % length ratio on a polymer-clad MZI can take sensitivity from 11 pm/K to <1 pm/K — at the cost of a spin-on, cure and adhesion step.

08

The 9 µm cliff

Mode mismatch · grating couplers · SSC · antireflection · assembly tolerances

A silicon waveguide mode is ~0.3 × 0.15 µm. A single-mode fibre mode is 10.4 µm in diameter. That is a 70× linear mismatch, and it is the reason coupling — not modulation, not detection — is where silicon photonics products are won and lost. The overlap integral between a Gaussian of waist w₁ and one of w₂ peaks when w₂/w₁ ≈ 1.12 and falls as exp[−2(w₂−w₁)²/(w₁²+w₂²)]; without help, a direct butt joint is a −14 dB event before you even count the Fresnel reflection.

Grating couplers solve the size problem by diffraction: a periodic (often apodised) grating etched into the top of the silicon scatters the guided mode upward. Each tooth reflects a small amplitude; the array sums coherently into a first-order beam whose far-field is deliberately made near-Gaussian, so a plain fibre above it couples well. Blazed two-level gratings reach 1.5–3 dB per coupler over a 40–60 nm band with ~65–85 % peak efficiency, and they can be placed anywhere on the die — which is what makes wafer-level optical probing possible and is arguably the platform's greatest manufacturing advantage. The costs: wavelength and angle sensitivity (the peak is ~1 nm wide unless apodised), polarisation dependence, and a back-reflection that must be suppressed by a deep trench or absorber.

Edge coupling is the low-loss, narrow-band alternative. A spot-size converter expands the mode in two stages — silicon → a wide thin SiN or Si tail (vertical expansion), then a long taper into the buried oxide / cladding (horizontal expansion) — until the mode is ~2–3 µm wide and matches a fibre with a high-index glass tip (n ≈ 2.0, pitch-matched) that demagnifies the fibre mode onto the chip. Best-in-class: 0.4–1.0 dB per facet, <0.1 dB return loss with a 2D antireflection angle and index-matched adhesive, over the full C+L band. The costs: you must expose the die edge, you must dicing-define or cleave a good facet, and the alignment tolerance is tight — ±0.5 µm for 0.5 dB of extra loss.

Reflection is a laser-killer. A bare silicon facet is R = ((3.48−1)/(3.48+1))² ≈ 30 % (−5.2 dB). Feedback of even −40 dB into a DFB destabilises its linewidth, increases RIN and can cause coherence-collapse chirp. The defences: 8° angled facets, two-layer broadband AR coatings (<0.5 % over 1260–1650 nm), index-matched optical adhesive, and grating designs with a back-reflection-suppressing trench.

Assembly is the bill of materials. In a pluggable transceiver the passive alignment — 8 to 16 fibre arrays at ±0.5 µm, epoxied, cured, and stable through −40→85 °C cycling and 260 °C reflow — can be 25–40 % of unit cost and the dominant yield risk. This is why the industry is pushing towards passive alignment with moulded fiducials, self-aligned laser blocks, and detachable / optical-chiplet architectures where the photonics wafer is tested optically before the fibre is ever touched.
Two ways to lose 3 dB. Vertical grating: wafer-probeable, broadband-ish, tolerant to ±1 µm. Edge with a spot-size converter: lowest loss, needs a facet, and a sub-micron assembly window. The tolerance panel plots coupling loss against lateral, vertical and angular misalignment.

Loss from mode mismatch alone: L = −10·log₁₀[ 4w₁²w₂² / (w₁²+w₂²)² ]. For 0.3 µm into 5.2 µm that is −14 dB. Expanding the chip mode to 1.6 µm with a two-stage SSC brings it to −1.4 dB — the entire value of the spot-size converter in one line of algebra.

Passive alignment to etched fiducials; UV-then-thermal dual-cure epoxy with low shrinkage; index-matched adhesive to kill the facet; a compliant intermediate carrier that absorbs CTE mismatch; and a laser-reflowable or thermo-compression join so the module can be reworked before final cure.

09

Making it repeatable

Layers · DRC · PCell library · corners · Monte Carlo · compact models · GU

A silicon photonics PDK is a much harder document than a CMOS one, because a photonic device is defined by its field, not by its transistor geometry, and the field is sensitive to things a logic PDK never tracks: side-wall angle, slab thickness, layer stacking order, and the difference between an annealed and un-annealed oxide.

The layer set is a stack of masks that must be read as a stack. A typical 0.18–0.25 µm-class silicon photonics process: ACT (silicon device layer, patterned to waveguide and slab), P1/P2 (PN junction implants), GE (germanium growth opening), SLAB or SHALLOW (a partial etch for passives and heater isolation), HEATER/RES (TiN or NiCr), VIA/M1..Mn (the logic metal stack), PAD (Al or Cu pill), plus SiN and polymer/SOC optional layers and a backside BKO-thinning step for vertical gratings and backside lasers.

Photonics DRC is its own discipline. Beyond width/space, a photonics runset enforces minimum bend radius, taper slope, smoothing of polygon vertices (a 1 nm stair-step on a sidewall is a scatterer), density of grating teeth, heater keep-out, Ge-to-Si alignment, and etch-region enclosure. Most foundries ship a separate .runcalc or Calibre photonics runset with the electrical one, and a tapeout that passes only the electronic DRC is not a valid photonics tapeout.

The library is delivered as PCells — parametric cells in the layout tool — because a photonic device's shape is a function of its physics. A good PCell does more than draw: it computes the length you asked for into the physical path length along a curved waveguide, generates the taper polygons, inserts the anchors, applies the dopant with the right enclosure, places the contacts, and refuses parameters outside the validated range. A great PCell writes a compact-model netlist node for the same instance so the same object exists in the schematic, the layout and the simulation.

Corners and Monte Carlo are where a photonic design either survives or does not. The corner variables are not just resistance and capacitance: they are w (waveguide width bias), t (device-layer thickness), nSi, side-wall angle, slab depth, junction sheet resistance, and grating etch depth. A ±10 nm width bias shifts neff by ~10⁻³ — a full microring resonance shift. So the correct question is not "does the nominal design work" but "what is the yield of a 40-channel demux", which is a Monte Carlo over correlated and uncorrelated components of the width error, run with 200–1000 samples and reported as a distribution, not a worst case.

GU — "guaranteed unit". The photonics equivalent of a standard-cell's characterization. A GU device is one whose performance envelope has been measured across a dedicated test-chip matrix (dozens of geometries × dozens of scribes) and is contractually guaranteed by the foundry across the process window. Build your design out of GU devices, and put every custom-drawn device on a test vehicle with probes, or you are taping out a measurement, not a product.
Four artefacts a photonics designer lives in. The mask stack as it is actually grown and etched; the rules that keep a layout manufacturable; the yield distribution that decides whether the design ships; and the Verilog-A / compact model that lets the circuit simulator see the light.

10

The supply chain

Foundries · integration styles · 200 vs 300 mm · cost model · ITAR

Silicon photonics matured because it inherited fabs rather than inventing them. The first generation ran on 0.18–0.25 µm logic lines with a photonics module bolted on: an extra silicon device layer, a Ge epi module, a SiN layer, a heater metal, and a fibre-coupling module at the back end. That template is now offered by a dozen fabs on three continents, and the differences between them are less about lithography than about which integration style they committed to.

The axes that matter commercially: does the platform carry a laser, and how; is there a Ge detector module; is there a thick-SiN low-loss layer for routing and delay lines; what is the metal stack (a 0.18 µm line with 3 layers of Al is a very different package design than a 45 nm line with Cu low-k and 11 layers); is there a flip-chip / fan-out / 2.5D interposer path; and is the process on 200 or 300 mm, which is roughly a 2.3× die-count swing in cost per part.

Cost behaves like CMOS, with one wrinkle. A photonics die is large and low-density — a 4×4 mm die with 40 optical devices is mostly waveguide — so its cost is set by wafer price over area, not by gate count. A 300 mm wafer at a mature-node price yields a transceiver die for a few dollars, at which point the laser, the fibre array and the assembly dominate, not the silicon. This is why the strategic fights in this industry are over laser integration and coupling/assembly, not over the photonics wafer.

Two structural facts shape sourcing. First, photonics is geographically concentrated and export-controlled: laser epitaxy, certain GaAs/InP flows and high-power photonic assemblies touch ITAR/ECCN categories, which is why dual-region sourcing is a design-time constraint, not a procurement afterthought. Second, photonics capacity is not fungible: a line with a Ge module and an InP bond tool cannot be swapped for a line without them, so a product designed for one platform is a multi-year commitment to it.

Design for the platform, not the idea. Ask four questions of any foundry before you commit an architecture: what is the guaranteed waveguide loss and neff variation across the wafer; what is the laser story and its qualification data; what is the coupling story and its wafer-probe coverage; and what test structures ship on every scribe. The answers decide your yield model more than any circuit technique.
Where the money goes. For an 800G DR transceiver the silicon photonic die is a small slice of the bill; the laser, the fibre-array unit, the alignment and the test are the bulk. Fix the assembly, not the waveguide.

High-power emitters, certain III-V epi and phased-array photonic beamformers fall under export control. If your product touches LiDAR or sensing, get the classification before the architecture, not after.

Measuring light on a wafer

Wafer probe · cutback · dither lock · burn-in · KGD · COC

A photonic die cannot be tested like a logic die, because the probe that touches it must carry photons as well as electrons, and because a photonic measurement is a wavelength-resolved measurement. The entire test strategy of a silicon photonics product follows from one question: at what point in the flow do you get optical access?

Wafer-level optical probing answers it with grating couplers. A fibre array on a goniometer head sits above the wafer at an 8–20° angle, aligned to the grating array with sub-micron accuracy, and a tunable laser sweeps across the die. From one probe card you can extract waveguide loss, neff maps, resonance wavelengths and Qs, coupling loss, PD responsivity and dark current, and heater π-powers — and, crucially, you can do it before the die is sawn, packaged and coupled to a fibre at 30 % of the module's cost. This is the single largest economic argument for grating couplers.

The workhorse extraction structures are geometric. Cutback: a family of waveguides of increasing length with identical couplers; fit transmission versus length and the slope is dB/cm, free of coupling uncertainty. Ring matrices: dozens of rings of different radii give resonance wavelength versus radius, hence ng, group index variation, and the wafer map of thickness. Fringer/MZI ladders give dispersion and loss simultaneously. TLM and circular TLM give contact resistance and sheet resistance for the junctions.

Active devices need a servo in the test. A microring's resonance must be locked before it can be measured, and the standard technique is dither lock: superimpose a small sinusoidal temperature or phase modulation, demodulate the monitor signal at the dither frequency to get the slope of the resonance, and integrate that error onto a heater until the slope signal nulls. The same loop, implemented in the product's firmware, is what keeps a 40-wavelength link aligned over temperature and life.

At the module level the tests are the telecom ones: eye opening (TEM or a real-time sampler), TDECQ for PAM4 — the vertical eye-closure penalty including noise, defined in IEEE 802.3 — OMAT, ER, RIN, side-mode suppression, wavelength accuracy and drift, optical return loss tolerance, receiver sensitivity and Stressed Eye Sensitivity, and Jitter decomposition into Rj/Dj/Sj. Reliability qualification is GR-468 / GR-1221 for telco and AEC-Q102 for optoelectronics, with laser burn-in at high current density and high temperature to push defective devices out of the population before they ship.

Known-optical-good-die. In a co-packaged or chiplet architecture you cannot afford to discover a bad photonic die after it is on the interposer. The flow therefore needs: wafer-level optical probe → laser burn-in and re-probe → known-good-laser-die → assembly → post-bond re-probe through a test access point on the package. Budget the test access structures at architecture time; they cost die area and they save the package.

Where it ships

Pluggable · CPO · OIO · coherent · LiDAR · FMCW · quantum · sensing

The pluggable transceiver is the incumbent and the largest volume. A DSP, a driver, a TIA and a silicon photonics die with an integrated laser sit in an OIF form factor (QSFP-DD, OSFP) and speak 400G-DR4, 800G-DR8 or 1.6T. Its virtues are interoperability, field replaceability and a mature supply chain; its vice is that the electrical channel from the switch ASIC to the CAGE eats 15–25 dB, and the DSP that recovers it burns 8–15 W per 800G module. The LPO / LRO variants (linear / semi-retimed optics) delete most of that DSP — only viable because the silicon photonics modulator is linear enough and the TIA is good enough.

Co-packaged optics (CPO) moves the optical engine from the faceplate to the substrate, next to the switch die, in the same package, connected by a couple of centimetres of trace instead of a couple of decibels of channel. The power saving is enormous — 5–8 pJ/bit versus 15–20 pJ/bit for a DSP-based pluggable — and it is the reason essentially every large switch vendor has a CPO program. The cost is architectural: the laser must live somewhere that is not the hottest die (external laser modules with fibre pigtails are the norm), the optical connections become permanent, serviceability changes, and the thermal/mechanical co-design of the package becomes the critical path.

Optical I/O for accelerators goes further: an optical chiplet bump-mounted or interposer-attached to a GPU/TPU, replacing Serdes with light to break the rack-scale bandwidth wall. This is where monolithic laser integration and µTP earn their keep, and where the reliability argument (a laser you cannot replace, next to a 700 W die) is the hardest open problem.

Coherent-lite (400ZR/800ZR) puts a full coherent transmit-receive — two pols, I/Q modulators, 90° hybrids, balanced detectors, a narrow-linewidth laser — on one silicon photonics die, driving 80–120 km of fibre from a pluggable. It is the most demanding design in the industry and the best fit for silicon's love of matched, balanced, phase-stable structures.

FMCW LiDAR on a chip uses the things silicon photonics is uniquely good at: a tunable narrow-linewidth laser, a delay-line interferometer, a broad optical bandwidth, and massively parallel solid-state beam steering via an optical phased array (thousands of antenna elements, each with a phase shifter). The same die provides velocity and range by chirp interferometry. The engineering is in the laser linewidth, the beam quality of the 2D grating emitter, and the thermal crosstalk of a 1024-heater steering matrix.

Quantum and cryogenic photonics uses silicon's low microwave loss, its CMOS reproducibility and its small size to make waveguide circuits for photonic qubits — beam-splitter meshes, interferometers, and cryo-CMOS drivers that must work at 4 K where silicon's carrier freeze-out changes the device physics. Biosensing and gyroscopes use the same resonators as gas sensors and as interferometric rotation sensors, where the low-loss SiN layer and the high-Q ring are the product.

The honest summary. Silicon photonics is not a faster-copper replacement; it is a conversion-economics play. It wins wherever you can amortise three opto-electronic conversions over enough bandwidth and enough distance — and it loses, decisively, wherever a copper trace still closes the link budget. The whole engineering discipline is in making the conversions cheap, the waveguide network passive and yield-limited, and the assembly an automated one.
Five products, one process. Each block is a device from the earlier chapters; what changes is which physics you exploit and which failure mode you are obliged to engineer out.

Design checklist — the twelve things that actually kill a SiPh product

1 · Laser

Where is it, can it be replaced, what is its qualification data, and how far is it from the hottest die? If the answer is "bonded, no, none, 2 mm" you have a reliability program, not a product.

2 · Coupling

One coupling scheme, chosen for the assembly line you actually have. Grating for wafer-probe and tolerance; edge+SSC for loss and bandwidth. Never both, unless you have a reason and a budget.

3 · Reflection

Every facet, every interface, every 90° mirror. Compute the feedback into the laser. Add trenches, angles, AR, absorbers. This is a two-week task that saves a two-year debug.

4 · Thermal

Map every heater's influence on every resonator. A 1024-heater switch matrix has a crosstalk matrix; measure it, model it, and constrain it in the floorplan.

5 · Variation

Monte Carlo the whole design over wafer-mapped width and thickness. Report yield, not worst case. Identify which devices you will trim and how much trim authority you need.

6 · Servo

Every resonator and every interferometer needs a lock. Budget the monitor PDs, the taps, the ADC channels, the DAC channels, the firmware and the calibration time. It is not an afterthought.

7 · Test access

Wafer-probe pads for optics and electronics on every block. A post-bond re-probe point in the package. If you cannot measure it after assembly, you will not yield.

8 · Packaging

CTE from InP to Si to substrate to PCB. Adhesive shrinkage. Fibre pull-out. Reflow profile vs. polymer glass transition. Sign the mechanical engineer up early.

9 · RF

Velocity match, 50 Ω, via stubs, and the loss of the metal stack you inherited from a logic process. The modulator is only as fast as its electrode.

10 · Reliability

AEC-Q102 / GR-468. Moisture hermeticity for polymer-clad and athermal devices. Electromigration in heater metals at 10⁵ hours. Adhesion of the AR coating.

11 · Cost

Model the unit cost as laser + fibre array + assembly + test + die. Then attack the biggest term, which is almost never the die.

12 · Sourcing

Two platforms for anything with a laser in it. Export-control classification before architecture. Long-lead laser and SiN capacity booked at design start.
